# ASD-STE100 Issue 9 writing rules: summaries

This file gives the 53 writing rules and the 8 general recommendations of
ASD-STE100 Issue 9. Each heading gives the rule number, a short paraphrase of
the rule title in this skill's words, and the page of the published standard.
The notes under each heading are this skill's own summaries, and the examples
are this skill's own. They are not the text of the standard.

The skill applies Issue 9 from these summaries. The official standard is the
authority. ASD supplies it free of charge on request from
https://asd-ste100.org. A user who has the standard should use it where it
and this skill do not agree. If the user shows you such a difference, follow
the standard and say so.

## Section 1: Words (p.45)

### Rule 1.1 Restrict the vocabulary to dictionary-approved words, technical nouns, and technical verbs. (p.46)

Part 2 of the standard is a controlled dictionary of the words that technical
writers need most. A word that the dictionary does not approve is permitted
only as a technical noun (Rule 1.5) or a technical verb (Rule 1.12). A
technical noun or technical verb is a term for a specific concept or process
in a subject field. These terms are usually in the company glossary or
terminology database: use that source together with the rules of Section 1.
The dictionary also lists a selection of words that are not approved, each
with approved alternatives.

### Rule 1.2 Give each approved word only the part of speech that the dictionary lists for it. (p.46)

The dictionary gives each approved word with its part of speech. Use the
word only as that part of speech.

- TEST is an approved noun, not a verb. Not STE: "Test the pump for leaks."
  STE: "Do a test of the pump for leaks."
- Some words have two approved parts of speech, for example CLEAN (v) and
  CLEAN (adj). The position of the word shows which one it is.
- If an alternative has the same part of speech, you can replace the word
  directly: "acceptable" (adj) becomes PERMITTED (adj).
- If the alternative has a different part of speech, change the sentence:
  "operable" (adj) has the alternative OPERATE (v), so write "Make sure that
  the pump can operate", not "is operable".
- Make sure that the alternative keeps the meaning. If it does not, select a
  different alternative or a different sentence construction.
- For a word that is not in the dictionary: find its meaning in a general
  English dictionary, find an approved STE word with that meaning, and use it
  or write the sentence again with other approved words.

### Rule 1.3 Give each approved word only the meaning that the dictionary assigns to it. (p.48)

Each approved word has one approved meaning, and that meaning can be
narrower than in general English. Use no other meaning. FOLLOW (v) means to
come after or go after. For the meaning "do what the instructions say", use
OBEY (v). Not STE: "Follow the instructions on the label." STE: "Obey the
instructions on the label."

### Rule 1.4 Inflect verbs and adjectives only in the forms that the dictionary permits. (p.48)

The dictionary gives the permitted forms with each approved verb, for
example REMOVE (v), REMOVES, REMOVED, REMOVED: the base form (infinitive and
imperative), the simple present, the simple past, and the past participle
(as an adjective). It gives the comparative and superlative of an adjective
in parentheses, for example SLOW (adj) (SLOWER, SLOWEST). An adjective that
uses "more" and "most" has no forms in the dictionary, because MORE and MOST
are approved words.

### Rule 1.5 A word that fits a technical-noun category is permitted. (p.49)

The dictionary does not list technical nouns, because each subject field has
too many of them. You can use a technical noun in procedural and descriptive
text if it fits one or more of these 22 categories (p.49-52):

1. Official parts information
2. Vehicles or machines, and locations on them
3. Tools and support equipment, their parts, and locations on them
4. Materials, consumables, and unwanted material
5. Facilities, infrastructure, and logistic procedures
6. Systems, components and circuits, their functions, configurations, and parts
7. Mathematical, scientific, engineering terms, and formulas
8. Navigation and geographic terms
9. Numbers, units of measurement and time (and their symbols)
10. Quoted text
11. Professional roles, individuals, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities
12. Parts of the body
13. Common personal effects, food, and beverages
14. Medical terms
15. Official documents, parts of documentation, standards, and guidelines
16. Environmental and operational conditions
17. Colors
18. Damage terms
19. Computer science, information and communication technology
20. Civil and military operations
21. Law and regulations
22. Animals, plants, and other life forms

Category 10 (p.51) is text that the writer cannot change: the words on placards,
labels, signs, markings, and displays. Category 17: colors are adjectives,
but STE treats them as technical nouns, and their comparative and
superlative forms ("redder", "the darkest") are not permitted. The standard
gives example words for each category. They are examples only, not a full
list (p.53).

### Rule 1.6 An unapproved word is permitted only as a technical noun or inside one. (p.53)

Some words that the dictionary lists as not approved can still be technical
nouns, or parts of technical nouns, when they fit a category. "Main" is not
approved (use PRIMARY): write "the primary parts of the pump". But keep
"main" in an approved technical noun such as "main bearing", because
"primary bearing" is not the term that the industry uses. A technical noun can
also contain a word that the dictionary does not list.

### Rule 1.7 Never turn a technical noun into a verb. (p.55)

Use a technical noun only as a noun, or as a modifier in a different
technical noun. Do not make it a verb. Write "Apply grease to the hinge",
not "Grease the hinge". A word can fit a technical noun category and a
technical verb category, and then it can be the noun in one context and the
verb in a different context ("drill": a process that removes material, and
also the tool).

### Rule 1.8 Prefer the technical nouns that your organization or field has already sanctioned. (p.55)

When your company, industry, or subject field has an approved term for a
system, component, part, or process, use that term. Such terms are usually
in the official parts data and the company documentation.

### Rule 1.9 Choose the shortest, clearest technical noun available. (p.56)

When no approved term exists, select one of three words or fewer that is
easy to understand. When an index number and an illustration identify a
part, a short noun ("the screws (4)") is sufficient. When they do not, add
one or two adjectives to help the reader.

### Rule 1.10 Keep regional terms, slang, and jargon out of technical nouns. (p.56)

Do not use a term that is known only in one region, or slang or jargon that
only a small group understands. Select well-known words. For example, write
"tools and equipment", not "kit" or "gear", for the general tools that a task needs. A kit that the parts data names is a technical noun (Rule 1.5, category 1).

### Rule 1.11 Call each item by a single technical noun throughout. (p.57)

When you select a technical noun for an item, use that noun each time that
the text refers to the item. Use the approved term of your company or field.
If one text calls the same part "pump", "unit", and "motor assembly", select
one of these terms and use only that term.

### Rule 1.12 A word that fits a technical-verb category is permitted. (p.57)

The dictionary does not list technical verbs. A technical verb is a term for
a specific process in a subject field, and it usually comes from the
glossary. Technical verbs obey all the rules of Section 3. There are four
categories, with these subgroups (p.57-58):

1. Manufacturing processes: (a) remove material, (b) add material,
   (c) attach material, (d) change the strength, structure, or physical
   properties of a material, (e) change the surface finish, (f) change the
   shape.
2. Computer processes and applications: (a) input and output, (b) user
   interface and application processes, (c) system operations.
3. Instructions and information for applicable subject fields:
   (a) engineering, mathematics, and science, (b) medicine, (c) civil and
   military operations, (d) navigation, (e) automotive and railway,
   (f) energy, oil, and gas.
4. Law and regulations: only in legal and regulatory texts.

The standard's example verbs are not a full list (p.59). For example, it
gives "convert" and "detect" in category 3 (a), and "notify" and
"comply with" in category 4.

Limits on technical verbs (p.59-60):

- The test comes first: if an approved verb is accurate in the sentence, the
  word is not a technical verb there. Use the approved verb. "Reset the
  indicator" becomes "Set the indicator" (reset (v) → SET, p.360), and "prime
  the pump" becomes "fill the pump" (prime (v) → FILL, p.339). Do not keep an
  unapproved verb only because it is a usual term in your industry.
- If an approved verb gives the instruction or the information accurately,
  use the approved verb, not a technical verb. Write "If you find loose
  wires", not "If you detect loose wires".
- If you must use a technical verb, use one that is precise in your context,
  not a general or unclear one.
- Do not use a technical verb when an approved verb and a technical noun can
  give the same information. Write "Put a clamp on the hose", not "Clamp the
  hose".
- Some words that the dictionary does not approve can be technical verbs,
  but only in the context of their category. "Enter your password" is
  correct (category 2 a). For a room, write "go into", not "enter".

### Rule 1.13 Never turn a technical verb into a noun. (p.60)

Use a technical verb only as a verb. Write "Ream the hole to 6 mm", not
"Give the hole a 6 mm ream". The past participle of a technical verb can be
an adjective ("the reamed hole"). A word can be a technical verb in one
context and a technical noun in a different context.

### Rule 1.14 Spell in American English unless an official directive says otherwise. (p.61)

Use the spelling of the STE dictionary, which is American English ("color",
"aluminum", "meter"). Use a different spelling only when a technical
publication specification, a style guide, a contract, or a different
official directive tells you to. Do not change the spelling of quoted text,
for example the text on a screen (Rule 8.6).

## Section 2: Multi-word nouns (p.63)

### Rule 2.1 Limit a noun cluster to three words. (p.63)

A multi-word noun is a group of nouns and adjectives that has the function
of one noun, usually the subject or the object. The head noun is usually the
last word. In a long group the reader cannot easily see how the words
connect, and some languages put the head noun first. Thus, a multi-word noun
can have a maximum of three words. Count the nouns and adjectives in the
group. A hyphenated group counts as one word (Rule 8.7).

The limit applies to the groups that you make. An approved technical noun of
more than three words (for example a name from the parts catalog) stays
whole: do not divide it to obey this rule. Rule 2.2 tells you how to write
it. If you add words to such a noun, the added words obey this rule: write
"the installation procedure for the <noun>", not "the <noun> installation
procedure".

To make a group shorter, use prepositions (for example "of", "on", "in",
"for") or a clause:

- Not STE: "Replace the pump inlet filter seal." (4 words)
  STE: "Replace the seal of the filter in the pump inlet." (1 word, 1 word,
  and 2 words)
- Not STE: "Remove the gearbox cover attachment screws." (4 words)
  STE: "Remove the screws that attach the cover to the gearbox."

### Rule 2.2 A technical noun of four or more words may be made clearer with a shorter form or with hyphens. (p.64)

The rule gives two methods. They are options: select the one that makes the
noun clear.

- Use a shorter form of the technical noun.
- Put hyphens (-) between the words that operate as one unit.

The usual result is: write the noun in full the first time. Then, each
time after that, use a shorter form or an abbreviation that is approved.
This obeys the rule. Writing the full noun each time also obeys it, but
it is harder to read, so prefer the shorter form after the first use. Make the shorter form from words of the noun
(three words or fewer), and at the first use show which noun it is, for
example in parentheses (Rule 8.3). Do not add a word that is not in the
noun. If a shorter form could also refer to a different item in the text,
keep the full noun and tell the user.

Many long multi-word nouns are approved technical nouns, or combinations of
them, and you cannot divide them. Write each in its approved form.

Shorter form (p.64-65). Write a long official term, for example from an
engineering drawing or a parts catalog, in full the first time that it
occurs. If possible, explain it. Then, in the rest of the document, use a
shorter form or an abbreviation that is approved. A term of three words or fewer
needs no abbreviation. You can use abbreviations from the official company
documentation, but carefully: a procedure full of abbreviations is shorter
but not easy to read.

Hyphens (p.66). Hyphens show which words operate as one unit, and a
hyphenated group counts as one word. Do not connect words that are not
related, because the hyphen then changes the meaning. If you are not sure,
explain the noun, and then use a shorter form or an abbreviation that is
approved. A hyphenated group must not have more than three words. Do not add hyphens
to a term of three words or fewer. Do not remove a hyphen that is part of an
approved technical noun.

If the source does not show how the words of a long noun connect, keep the
noun and write `[needs: meaning of "<noun>"]` (see `SKILL.md`).

## Section 3: Verbs (p.67)

### Rule 3.1 Restrict verbs to the forms that the dictionary lists. (p.67)

Each approved verb in the dictionary comes with the forms that you can use,
for example GIVE (v), GIVES, GAVE, GIVEN. Use only these forms.

### Rule 3.2 Limit verbs to six permitted forms and tenses. (p.68)

The permitted forms and tenses are six:

- The infinitive ("to clean")
- The imperative, or command form ("Clean the filter.")
- The simple present ("The pump operates.")
- The simple past ("The pump operated.")
- The simple future ("The pump will operate.")
- The past participle, used only as an adjective ("the cleaned filter")

All other forms and tenses are not permitted. For example: the present
perfect ("has cleaned"), the past perfect ("had cleaned"), the present and
past progressive ("is cleaning", "was cleaning"), and all other complex verb
constructions. Two notes:

- Rule 3.6 is the one exception: in descriptive text, a simple passive
  ("Oil is injected into the airend.") is permitted when the agent is
  unknown.
- A modal verb with an infinitive is permitted: "You can adjust the
  screw", "The pump must operate". CAN, MUST, and WILL are approved
  (dictionary). Rule 3.4 forbids a modal with "be" and a past participle.

### Rule 3.3 A past participle may serve as an adjective. (p.68)

A past participle used as an adjective shows the condition of something.
This is not the passive voice. Put it before a noun ("the drained tank"), or
after a form of "to be", "to become", or "to stay" ("When the tank is
drained, ..."). Use only a past participle that the dictionary gives. Some
approved adjectives, such as PERMITTED and DAMAGED, have the form of a past
participle of a verb that is not approved. You can use them as adjectives.

### Rule 3.4 Avoid chains of auxiliary verbs that build complex verb phrases. (p.69)

Three cases:

- "have", "has", or "had" with a past participle makes a perfect tense. It
  is not permitted.
- "is to be", or a modal ("can", "must", "will") with "be" and a past
  participle, makes a complex passive construction. It is not permitted.
- A plain "is", "are", "was", or "were" with a past participle is not
  forbidden by this rule. Decide what it is. If it gives the condition of an
  item ("When the tank is drained, ..."), it is an adjective (Rule 3.3). If
  it gives an action ("The tank is drained by the operator"), it is a simple
  passive, and Rule 3.6 decides if you can keep it.

- Not STE: "The technician has replaced the fuse." STE: "The technician
  replaced the fuse."
- Not STE: "The filter can be removed." STE: "You can remove the filter."
- Not STE: "The pressure must be measured." STE: "Measure the pressure."

### Rule 3.5 Limit "-ing" words to technical nouns and their modifiers. (p.70)

An "-ing" word can be a verb, an adjective, a noun, or the start of a long
phrase, and this causes ambiguity and long sentences. Thus, "-ing" words are
usually not permitted.

- Not STE: "While removing the cover, hold the spring."
- STE: "When you remove the cover, hold the spring."

Permitted (p.71): an "-ing" word that is a technical noun, for example a
heading such as "Troubleshooting" or "Cleaning", and an "-ing" modifier in a
technical noun that tells the function of the item ("cutting tool", "mixing
valve"). A process name such as "cleaning" or "handling" is a technical noun
only when it is the name of a defined process (for example in the glossary or
as a heading). In a sentence about the work, use the verb: "When the system
starts to clean the filters", not "When the cleaning starts". The approved
"-ing" words are LIGHTING, OPENING, ROUTING,
SERVICING (n), MATING, MISSING, REMAINING (adj), SOMETHING (pron), and
DURING (prep).

### Rule 3.6 Prefer the active voice. Descriptive text may use the passive only when the actor is unknown. (p.71)

In the active voice, the subject does the action. In the passive voice, the
subject receives the action, and the agent (the person or thing that does
the action) is after "by" or is not given. To find a passive, ask "by whom or
by what?" (p.72). The passive is permitted only in descriptive writing, and
only when the agent is unknown. Do not make an incorrect thing the agent. If
the agent is unknown, keep the passive, or use SOMETHING as the agent (p.72).

There are four methods to change to the active voice (p.72-74):

1. If the sentence gives the agent, make the agent the subject. Not STE:
   "The valve is opened by a solenoid." STE: "A solenoid opens the valve."
2. Change an infinitive construction to an active verb. Not STE: "The sensor
   data is used by the controller to set the fan speed." STE: "The controller
   sets the fan speed from the sensor data."
3. In procedures, use the imperative. Not STE: "The cover is to be removed."
   STE: "Remove the cover."
4. When the sentence does not give the agent, use "you" if the agent is the
   reader, or "we" if the agent is your company or organization. Not STE:
   "The filter can be cleaned with water." STE: "You can clean the filter
   with water."

For a complex passive with an auxiliary, decide if the sentence is
procedural (use the imperative) or descriptive (for example "You can ..." or
"The operator must ...").

Use "we" only if the source shows that the agent is the company. Do not
invent an agent (see `SKILL.md`).

How to apply the rule to a passive:

- Procedural text: use the active voice, usually the imperative (method 3).
- Descriptive text, agent unknown: keep the passive.
- Descriptive text, agent known: make the agent from the source the subject
  (method 1), or use "you" or "we" (method 4). You can also use a verb that
  needs no agent ("The valve opens").
- Never make an agent that the source does not give.

### Rule 3.7 Express an action with an approved verb, not with a noun or another word class. (p.75)

Different STE sentences can give the same information. When an approved
verb describes the action, use it, because a verb shows an action more
clearly than a noun. "The gauge shows 30 psi" is better than "The gauge
gives an indication of 30 psi", and "Before you remove the unit" is better
than "Before the removal of the unit". All four are STE, because INDICATION
and REMOVAL are approved nouns: the rule prefers the verb. If a word is not
approved as a verb, do not use it as a verb. Change the sentence: "Do a
check of the battery", not "Check the battery".

## Section 4: Sentences (p.77)

STE text must be easy to read and understand. The rules of Section 4 apply
to procedures and to descriptions.

### Rule 4.1 Keep sentences brief and unambiguous. (p.77)

Write short, clear sentences with accurate instructions and information.

- In a procedure, give short and direct instructions in the imperative.
  Divide a long instruction into steps.
- In a description, give each sentence only one topic and no imperative.
  Then add information about that topic in the sentences that follow.
- In the two types of text, do not write abstract statements. Show clearly
  how to do the task or how the system operates. Write "Make sure that the
  blade has no cracks", not "No cracks are permitted".
- Be accurate (p.78). Do not give information that is not correct, or that a
  reader can understand in two ways.

### Rule 4.2 Never drop words or contract them to save length. (p.79)

Each sentence must have all its parts. A shorter sentence is not easier to
read. Do not omit:

- A noun or a subject. Not STE: "If damaged, replace the gasket." STE: "If
  the gasket is damaged, replace it."
- A verb. Not STE: "Selector to AUTO." STE: "Set the selector to AUTO."
- An article. Not STE: "Remove the cap and seal." STE: "Remove the cap and
  the seal." (Without "the", "seal" can be a verb.)

Do not use contractions ("don't", "isn't", "can't"). Write the words in full:
"do not".

### Rule 4.3 Break complex text into a vertical list. (p.80)

When a sentence must give many items (for example parts, components, or
documents) or many actions, put them in a vertical list. When you make a
vertical list:

- Put a colon (:) after the text that introduces the list, before the first
  item.
- Identify each item with a number, a letter, a punctuation mark, or a
  symbol (for example a dash, a bullet, a letter, or a number). The
  applicable specification or style guide tells you which marks to use.
- Start each item with an uppercase letter.
- When the subject of an item is a noun, put an article before that noun
  where applicable ("The filter", not "Filter").
- Put a period at the end of an item only if the item is a full sentence. An
  item that is not a full sentence has no period, even if it contains a verb.
- An item does not end with a comma or a semicolon.
- Put a period at the end of the last item.
- Do not mix procedural and descriptive writing in one list (p.81).
- Make sure that each item connects clearly and correctly to the text
  before the colon (p.82).
- Keep all the items at one level. Give a subgroup in parentheses in its
  item, not as a second list in the list (p.82).
- In a safety instruction, put DO NOT in each item where it applies, not
  once in the text before the colon (p.81).

### Rule 4.4 Link sentences on related topics with connectors. (p.83)

A connecting word or phrase connects a topic in one sentence to an idea in
the sentence after it. In descriptive text, connecting words give a logical
structure. Approved connecting words include AND, BUT, THEN, and THUS.
Connecting phrases include "as a result" and "at the same time". "This" and
"these" can also connect sentences. In a procedure, use them where a work
step needs an explanation. In a safety instruction, use them to connect
related sentences or to make the text clear.

### Rule 4.5 Where it fits, put an article (the, a, an) or "this"/"these" before a noun or noun cluster. (p.84)

Articles and demonstrative adjectives show where the nouns are in the
sentence. Do not omit them to make the text shorter: "Close the valve", not
"Close valve".

- Do not use an article in a general statement or before an abstract
  concept: "Water can cause corrosion."
- In a short sentence, an article before each noun can be clearer. In a long
  series, one article before the first noun can be clearer. Then make sure
  that an adjective does not become ambiguous: "the new washers, nuts, and
  bolts" (all are new); "the new washers, the nuts, and the bolts" (only the
  washers are new).
- Do not put "the" before a noun that an alphanumeric identifier follows,
  because the identifier makes it a proper noun (p.85). Write "Open valve
  V12", not "Open the valve V12".

## Section 5: Procedural writing (p.87)

### Rule 5.1 Procedural sentences: 20 words at most. (p.87)

A procedural sentence has a maximum of 20 words. Warnings, cautions, and all
other safety instructions also obey this limit. Count words with Section 8.
When an instruction is too long, divide it into two sentences. If the two
actions occur at the same time, keep the two sentences in one work step
(p.88). Notes do not obey Rule 5.1: a note is descriptive, and each sentence
in it has a maximum of 25 words (p.88, Rule 5.5).

### Rule 5.2 One instruction per sentence, except for simultaneous actions. (p.88)

Give one instruction in each sentence, and show the sequence of the work
steps, usually with numbers or letters. A procedure can have as many work
steps as necessary.

- Not STE: "Disconnect the hose and remove the clamp."
- STE: "1. Disconnect the hose. 2. Remove the clamp."

You can give two actions in one sentence only when they occur at the same
time ("Push the lever and turn the handle." "Cut and remove the tie.").
A work step can have more than one sentence when actions occur at the same
time, or when a result occurs immediately after the action, for example a
measurement and its limit (p.89).

### Rule 5.3 Phrase each instruction as an imperative. (p.89)

An instruction tells the reader to do something, so its verb is in the
imperative: "Remove the cover." A different structure ("The cover must be
removed", "The cover can be removed") does not tell the reader if the step
is important, if a different person did it, or if a different person must do
it later.

Use "must" before an imperative in two cases only: the instruction is very
important for safety (as in a safety instruction), or it gives an important
condition (p.89-90). Write "Before you open the panel, disconnect
the power", not "Before you open the panel, you must disconnect the power".
Thus, "You should clean the filter" becomes "Clean the filter" (should (v):
MUST, IF, p.377).

### Rule 5.4 Put a condition the reader needs first at the start, as a descriptive clause set off from the command by a comma. (p.90)

When a work step needs a special condition, write the condition first and
put a comma between the condition and the command.

- Not STE: "Remove the cap when the pressure is zero."
- STE: "When the pressure is zero, remove the cap."

The position of the comma can change the meaning. Make sure that it shows
which verb an adverb modifies.

A clause with "before" or "after" that only gives the sequence of two
actions is not such a condition. It can stay after the command, as in the
dictionary's own STE examples ("prepare the mixture 20 minutes before you
use it", p.337; "you can operate the vehicle after the inspection is
completed", p.183). It can also come first (BEFORE (conj), AFTER (conj)).
Do not report its position as a Rule 5.4 finding. This carve-out is
this skill's reading of the dictionary examples. The standard's own rewrites
usually put a "before" clause first ("Before you ..., do ..."), so a "before"
clause at the start is never wrong. A condition that the
reader must check or wait for before the action ("if ...", "when ...")
comes first.

### Rule 5.5 Notes carry information only, never instructions. (p.91)

A note gives only information that helps the reader during a procedure. A
note is descriptive text and obeys the rules of Section 6. Each sentence in
a note has a maximum of 25 words.

- A note must not give an instruction, a requirement, or a limit, and must
  not use the imperative. Text in the imperative is a work step.
- If the information prevents damage or injury, give it in a safety
  instruction, not in a note (p.92).
- Give a limit, a tolerance, or a result in the work step, directly after the
  action, not in a note (p.92).
- Test (p.93): read the procedure without its notes. If the reader cannot do
  the procedure correctly, move the missing information from the note into a
  work step, and do the test again.
- In descriptive text, use a note only for an illustration or a table
  (p.93).

## Section 6: Descriptive writing (p.95)

Descriptive writing gives information, not instructions. The imperative is
not permitted. Descriptive writing is (p.95):

- A description of an item, product, system, or component, which tells its
  function, its construction, and its operation
- A text of general information, for example a report, a brochure, or a paper
- A note in a procedure.

### Rule 6.1 Release information in stages. (p.95)

Give information step by step, and give each sentence only one subject. Too
much information too quickly makes the reader read the text again.

### Rule 6.2 Structure the text with key words and phrases. (p.96)

Key words and key phrases occur again in the text to connect ideas and to
show how the information is related. Use the same key words each time. Do
not change them in your text. Connecting words (Rule 4.4) also help.

### Rule 6.3 Descriptive sentences: 25 words at most. (p.98)

A descriptive sentence has a maximum of 25 words. The limit is higher than
for procedures because descriptive text is more complex. Divide a longer
sentence, or use a vertical list (Rule 4.3).

### Rule 6.4 Group related information into paragraphs. (p.99)

Paragraphs keep related information together and put it in a logical
sequence. Each paragraph starts with a topic sentence that tells the reader
the topic. The sentences after it explain the topic or add information about
it.

### Rule 6.5 Limit each paragraph to a single topic. (p.100)

Each paragraph has one topic. The topic sentence is first and is the most
important sentence. It connects the new information to the previous
information, usually with a key word or a connecting word. The topic
sentences of a text, read together, give an outline of its content.

### Rule 6.6 Six sentences at most per paragraph. (p.101)

Divide a paragraph of seven or more sentences into two paragraphs.
Do not put different topics in one paragraph.

## Section 7: Safety instructions (p.103)

A safety instruction tells the reader that a procedure or a work step can be
dangerous or can cause damage. Descriptive text usually has no safety
instructions, unless they are quoted text or the description includes a
safety risk. In aerospace and defense specifications (p.103):

- A warning: a risk of injury or death.
- A caution: a risk of damage to objects.

Other industries can use other words (for example "danger", "attention", or
"notice") or graphical symbols. The content still obeys Rules 7.1 to 7.3.
ISO 45001, ANSI Z535, and ISO 3864 are references only.

### Rule 7.1 Mark the risk level with a signal word such as "warning" or "caution". (p.104)

Use a word, or a symbol where applicable, that shows the level of risk
immediately. Do a risk analysis, then:

- Risk of injury or death: use a warning.
- Risk of damage to machines, tools, or equipment: use a caution.
- The two risks together: use a warning.

Give clear information about the risk, not an abstract statement. STE gives
no rules for formatting.

If a safety instruction in the source has no signal word, select one from
the risk that the source text gives, with the test above (p.104). A burn or
a shock is a risk of injury: WARNING. Damage to a part only: CAUTION. Tell
the user that you added the signal word. If the source text does not show
the type of risk, write `[needs: level of risk]` in place of the signal word. The standard's examples are in uppercase, but
uppercase is not an STE rule.

### Rule 7.2 Open a safety instruction with a precise command or condition. (p.105)

Open with a precise command or condition, so that the reader knows how
to prevent an accident. If the reader must know a condition before
the procedure or the work step starts, give the condition first.

- Command first: "WARNING: DO NOT TOUCH THE TERMINALS. AN ELECTRICAL SHOCK
  CAN CAUSE INJURY OR DEATH."
- Condition first: "CAUTION: WHEN YOU INSTALL THE LENS, DO NOT TOUCH ITS
  SURFACE. OIL FROM YOUR SKIN CAN CAUSE DAMAGE TO THE LENS."

### Rule 7.3 Explain the risk or the likely consequence. (p.106)

If possible, tell the reader what can occur if the reader does not obey the
safety instruction. A clear and specific risk makes the person who does the
task more careful.

Take each part from the source only. For a part that the source does not
give, see `SKILL.md` Section 7.

## Section 8: Punctuation and word count (p.107)

STE gives no rules about general punctuation (p.107). For general
punctuation, use a reference book or style guide.

### Rule 8.1 Any standard English punctuation mark is permitted except the semicolon (;). (p.108)

The semicolon is not permitted, because it makes long sentences and is not
easy to use correctly. Write two sentences.

### Rule 8.2 Hyphenate (-) words that belong closely together. (p.108)

A hyphen connects words, or parts of words, that are directly related. Use
it in:

1. An adjective of two or more words before a noun ("heat-resistant sleeve",
   "quick-release pin")
2. A number or fraction of two words ("thirty-two", "three-quarters")
3. An uppercase letter or a number plus a noun, usually to show a shape or a
   configuration ("T-handle", "4-pin connector")
4. A verb whose first part is a noun or a different part of speech
   ("spot-weld")
5. A prefix that ends in a vowel before a root that starts with a vowel
   ("re-enter")

A dash is not a hyphen. A dash divides ideas, shows a range, or shows a
pause.

### Rule 8.3 Parentheses have seven permitted uses. (p.109)

The rule lists seven uses (p.109-110):

1. To refer to an illustration or to a part of the text ("Remove the cap
   (Figure 2).")
2. To give the letters or numbers that identify items in an illustration or
   a text ("the screws (4)")
3. To identify the work steps of a procedure ("(1)", "(2)")
4. To give an abbreviation ("residual current device (RCD)")
5. To give the singular and the plural of a noun together ("filter(s)")
6. To explain words or a part of a sentence ("Turn the knob slowly (one turn
   each second).")
7. To give an alternative ("Open the left (right) door.")

The rule lists the permitted uses. It does not say that other uses are
forbidden. If parentheses have a different use, report it as advisory, with
Rule 8.3, and do not count it as a violation.

### Rule 8.4 For the word count, a colon (:) that introduces list items ends a sentence, as a period does. (p.110)

The colon of a vertical list ends a sentence for word count. The text before
the colon has a maximum of 20 words in a procedure and 25 words in a
description. Each item after the colon is a new sentence, with the same
limit.

### Rule 8.5 Parenthesized text counts as a single word of its sentence. (p.111)

Everything inside the parentheses adds one word to its sentence's count. The words in the
parentheses also make a separate sentence, which you count separately. An
identifier or an abbreviation in parentheses counts as one word.

### Rule 8.6 Certain elements count as one word each. (p.111)

The elements are:

- A number ("13", "twenty-one")
- A number with its unit of measurement ("15 mm", "15 millimeters")
- An abbreviation, an acronym, or an initialism
- An alphanumeric identifier ("No. 2", "K4")
- Quoted text: words in quotation marks, or shown by uppercase letters or a
  different font. A formula is quoted text.
- A title, a heading, or the text on a placard or a label
- The proper noun of a person, a group, an organization, or a geopolitical
  entity

Do not count the numbers that identify paragraphs or work steps (p.112).

### Rule 8.7 A hyphenated compound counts once. (p.114)

A hyphenated group counts as one word. This applies to an adjective group
before a noun and to the hyphenated parts of a long technical noun.

## Section 9: Writing practices (p.115)

### Rule 9.1 Restructure the sentence when a one-for-one word swap does not work. (p.115)

When the dictionary gives an alternative with the same part of speech, you
can replace the word directly. Make sure that the meaning does not change.
Use a different sentence construction when (p.116-117):

1. The alternative needs a different grammatical structure. "Visible" (adj)
   becomes SEE (v): "make sure that you can see the gauge".
2. A direct replacement gives a sentence that has no clear meaning or is not
   good English.
3. The alternative changes the meaning.
4. The word is not in the dictionary. Find its meaning, then write the
   sentence with approved words.

Before you write the new sentence, ask what the word means in this context
and what action the reader must do. You can divide a long sentence, remove
information that is not necessary, or ask an engineer for more information.
When you change one part of a text, make sure that the change has no bad
effect on other parts (p.117-118). The new sentence must not change the
meaning. Where information is missing, do not invent it: write a
`[needs: …]` (see `SKILL.md`).

### Rule 9.2 Use every approved word as the dictionary intends. (p.119)

Many approved words have a restricted meaning. Before you use a word, read
its approved meaning, and use only that meaning and the approved part of
speech. For example: WEAR (v) means to become damaged by friction, so write
"Put on gloves", not "Wear gloves". TURN (v) is only for movement around an
axis or a point, so write "The color of the lamp changes to red", not "The
lamp turns red". SEE (v) is only for what the eyes see. For "find out", use
"make sure that". ABOVE and BELOW are for physical positions only. For
limits, use MORE THAN and LESS THAN (p.120). A word with two approved parts
of speech, such as FLUSH (v) and FLUSH (adj), has different positions in a
sentence for each (p.121).

### Rule 9.3 Do not form phrasal verbs from word pairs. (p.121)

A phrasal verb is a verb and a preposition that together have a new
meaning. It is not permitted, even when each word is approved. Write
"Extinguish the fire", not "put out the fire", and "can release fumes", not
"can give off fumes". The dictionary usually does not list phrasal verbs as
not approved. A small number of phrasal verbs, such as PUT ON and COME ON,
are approved, each with a restricted meaning (p.122).

### Rule 9.4 Keep terminology and wording consistent. (p.122)

Each time the same type of work step occurs, use the same terminology and
the same wording, and use the same noun for the same item. Different words
for the same action cause confusion and delays. In descriptive text, use
words consistently and give the text a logical structure (p.123).

## General recommendations (p.123)

These are not STE rules. They help you to prevent typical errors.

### GR-1 The conjunction "that" (p.123)

Use "that" after verbs such as "make sure" and "show" as much as possible
("Make sure that ..."). It shows where the main clause stops.

### GR-2 The preposition "with" (p.124)

WITH has three approved meanings. Make sure that it does not cause
ambiguity. When you rewrite a sentence that uses "with", keep the primary
action verb ("Seal the joint with tool T4", not "Use tool T4 to seal the
joint").

### GR-3 How to use pronouns (p.125)

Use only the pronouns in the dictionary. If a pronoun can refer to two
nouns, use the noun.

### GR-4 The pronoun "this" (p.125)

Make sure that the reader knows what "this" refers to.

### GR-5 False friends (p.126)

Use the English meaning of a word, not the meaning of a similar word in a
different language.

### GR-6 Latin abbreviations (p.126)

Do not use "e.g.", "i.e.", or "etc.". Write the English words, or omit them.

### GR-7 Inclusive language (p.127)

Use gender-neutral language. Do not use "he" or "she". Use "man" and "woman"
only where the context makes them necessary (for example, a medical text).

### GR-8 Possessive form (p.127)

The possessive form is permitted. If you are not sure that it is correct,
do not use it.

## Not regulated by STE

STE gives no rules for abbreviations, text formatting, or units of
measurement (p.36). Do not report an STE finding for these.

## Technical accuracy

Rules 4.1 and 9.1 require accurate text that keeps the meaning. Do not change
a technical noun, a part number, a measurement, a tolerance, or a value to
obey a rule. Do not remove safety data to make the text short. If a rule and
the technical accuracy have a conflict, keep the accuracy and tell the user.
